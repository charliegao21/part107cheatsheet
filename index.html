Where the points are

Operations is the biggest single bucket and it is the one people under-study, because it looks like common sense. It isn't — it's radio phraseology, airport traffic patterns, hazardous attitudes, physiology and maintenance. Airspace and chart reading is where most failures actually happen.

Test-day logistics what happens in the room

  • Test is given at an FAA-approved PSI knowledge testing center. Book ahead — slots go 1–2 weeks out.
  • Get an FAA Tracking Number (FTN) first by creating an IACRA profile at iacra.faa.gov. You need it to register for the test.
  • Bring government-issued photo ID with your name, date of birth, signature, and current address.
  • You get the FAA-CT-8080-2 Airman Knowledge Testing Supplement at the center — the booklet of legends, sectional excerpts and figures the questions refer to. Download the same PDF now and practice on it so it isn't new on test day.
  • No notes, phone, or smartwatch. Scratch paper and a basic calculator (non-programmable, memory cleared) are provided or permitted.
  • You get your score immediately on an Airman Knowledge Test Report (AKTR), listing the ACS codes you missed. Keep it — you need it for IACRA.
  • Fail? Wait 14 calendar days, then retest (pay again).

The certification path order matters

StepWhat you do
1Confirm eligibility: 16+, able to read/speak/write/understand English, in a physical and mental condition to operate safely.
2Create an IACRA account → get your FTN.
3Pass the UAG knowledge test at a PSI center.
4File FAA Form 8710-13 in IACRA using your AKTR / exam ID.

6Print the temporary certificate from IACRA (usually within days). Permanent card arrives by mail.

Staying current

The remote pilot certificate itself does not expire. To keep exercising its privileges you must complete free online recurrent training every 24 calendar months through the FAA Safety Team course catalog — ALC-677 is the course for Part 107 certificate holders. No retest, no fee. Pilots who also hold a Part 61 certificate with a current flight review take a different version of the course; check faasafety.gov for the current course number before you sit down.

Carry with you when you fly

Remote pilot certificate · photo ID · proof of aircraft registration · any waiver or airspace authorization you're operating under. All must be available to the FAA, NTSB, TSA or law enforcement on request — electronic copies are acceptable for registration.

Sheet 02 · Area of Operation I

Regulations

14 CFR Part 107 (operating rules) and Part 89 (Remote ID). Most of this area is straight recall — learn the numbers cold.

Applicability & definitions 107.1–107.3

  • Part 107 covers civil small unmanned aircraft systems: less than 55 lb total takeoff weight, including everything on board or attached.
  • sUAS = the aircraft plus control station, links, and support equipment. sUA = the aircraft alone.
  • Remote pilot in command (RPIC): directly responsible for and the final authority over the operation.
  • Person manipulating the controls: may fly under the direct supervision of an RPIC who can immediately take over.
  • Visual observer (VO): optional crewmember who helps see and avoid. Not required, but if used must be able to communicate with the RPIC.
  • Part 107 does not cover: recreational flyers under the exception for limited recreational operations (49 USC 44809), amateur rocketry, moored balloons/kites, public aircraft operations flown under a COA, air carrier operations.

Operating limitations 107.51 & friends — memorize

400 ftmax altitude AGL — or within 400 ft of a structure's uppermost limit
100 mphmax groundspeed (87 knots)
3 SMmin flight visibility from the control station
500 ftmin distance below clouds
2,000 ftmin horizontal distance from clouds
55 lbmax total takeoff weight
  • Visual line of sight (107.31): the RPIC and person manipulating controls must be able to see the aircraft with unaided vision throughout — enough to know its location, attitude, altitude and direction, see and avoid other aircraft, and confirm it doesn't endanger anyone. Corrective lenses are allowed; binoculars, telephoto and FPV goggles are not (a VO or spotter can enable FPV, but the RPIC/VO must maintain unaided VLOS).
  • One aircraft at a time (107.35): a person may not operate or act as RPIC for more than one sUA simultaneously.
  • Right of way (107.37): yield to all other aircraft. Never operate so close to another aircraft as to create a collision hazard.
  • Moving vehicles (107.25): no operating from a moving aircraft ever; from a moving land or waterborne vehicle only over a sparsely populated area (or over water). Carrying property for compensation from a moving vehicle is prohibited.
  • Hazardous operation (107.23): no dropping an object in a way that creates undue hazard to persons or property. Careless or reckless operation is prohibited — that's the catch-all the FAA enforces with.
  • Hazardous materials (107.36): carrying hazmat is prohibited — and this section cannot be waived.
  • Structures: the 400 ft AGL ceiling extends to 400 ft above a structure's uppermost limit when flying within a 400 ft radius of it — that's how tower and antenna inspections are legal.

Night & twilight operations 107.29

  • Night flight is allowed without a waiver if the aircraft has anti-collision lighting visible for at least 3 statute miles with a flash rate sufficient to avoid collision.
  • Same lighting requirement applies during civil twilight (the 30 minutes before official sunrise and after official sunset, except in Alaska).
  • The RPIC must have completed the initial knowledge test or recurrent training that covers night operations — the current curriculum includes it.
  • Preflight for night must include the lighting, and the crew briefing should cover reduced depth perception and how the site looks in the dark.

Operations over human beings 107 Subpart D — 107.100–107.165

Default rule: you may not fly over a person who is not directly participating, not under a covered structure, and not inside a stationary covered vehicle — unless the aircraft qualifies for a category.

CategoryQualifies ifOver open-air assembly?
1Weighs 0.55 lb (250 g) or less including everything on board, and has no exposed rotating parts that could lacerate human skinYes — only if the aircraft has Remote ID and it is broadcasting
2Impact would transfer no more than 11 ft-lb of kinetic energy to a human, no lacerating exposed parts, no safety defects; manufacturer files a Declaration of ComplianceYes — with Remote ID broadcasting

Two phrases that decide these questions

Sustained flight = hovering above, flying back and forth over, or circling over people. Merely transiting is treated differently. An open-air assembly is a gathering of people outdoors — a concert, a parade, a stadium crowd; the FAA judges it by density and how easily people could disperse, not by a headcount.

Operations over moving vehicles 107.145

  • Category 1 and 2 aircraft may operate over moving vehicles.
  • Category 3 may only do so within a closed- or restricted-access site where everyone inside has been notified.
  • Category 4 may, subject to its operating limitations.
  • No sustained flight over moving vehicles unless the operation is inside such a site or the people in the vehicles are participants.

Remote ID 14 CFR Part 89

Nearly every drone that requires registration must be able to broadcast Remote ID. Three ways to comply:

RouteWhat it means
Standard Remote IDBuilt into the aircraft at manufacture. Broadcasts from takeoff to shutdown.
Broadcast moduleAdd-on device attached to the aircraft. Requires flight within visual line of sight at all times; the module's serial number goes on your registration.
FRIAFAA-Recognized Identification Area — a defined site (usually a community-based organization or educational field) where non-equipped aircraft may fly, staying within VLOS and inside the boundaries.

Broadcast message contents: aircraft ID (serial or session ID); the UA's latitude, longitude, geometric altitude and velocity; the control station's latitude, longitude and geometric altitude (standard RID) or the takeoff location (broadcast module); a time mark; and emergency status. Broadcast is over unlicensed radio spectrum — anyone with a receiver can see it. No internet connection is required.

Registration & marking 107.13, Part 48

  • Register at FAA DroneZone. Part 107 aircraft register individually — $5 each, valid 3 years, and regardless of weight. The 0.55 lb exemption applies only to aircraft flown exclusively under the recreational exception (49 USC 44809); a 249 g drone flown for work still has to be registered.
  • You must be 13+ to register; owner must be a U.S. citizen or permanent resident.
  • The registration number must be legible on the exterior surface of the aircraft.
  • Proof of registration must be available to authorities during operation (paper or electronic).
  • Registration status changes (sale, destruction, address change) must be reported to the FAA.

Accident reporting 107.9 — classic exam question

Within 10 calendar days

Report to the FAA if the operation results in either:

  • Serious injury to any person, or any loss of consciousness — the FAA benchmark is an injury requiring hospitalization, equivalent to Level 3 or higher on the Abbreviated Injury Scale.
  • Property damage of at least $500 to repair or replace, at fair market value, to property other than the small unmanned aircraft itself.

Report through FAA DroneZone or the local Flight Standards District Office (FSDO). NTSB notification is separate and applies to more serious events.

Alcohol & drugs 107.27, 91.17, 91.19

  • No operating within 8 hours of consuming alcohol ("8 hours bottle to throttle").
  • No operating with a blood alcohol concentration of 0.04 or greater.
  • No operating while under the influence of any drug that affects faculties in a way contrary to safety — that includes legal prescriptions and over-the-counter antihistamines.
  • Refusing a test or a conviction for a drug/alcohol offense is grounds for denial or revocation of the certificate.

Preflight & condition for safe operation 107.15, 107.49

  • No airworthiness certificate is required, but the RPIC must determine the sUAS is in a condition for safe operation before and during flight, and must discontinue the flight if it isn't.
  • Required preflight actions: assess the operating environment (local weather, airspace and flight restrictions, location of persons and property, other hazards); brief participants on operating conditions, emergency procedures, contingencies, roles and hazards; verify all links work; confirm sufficient power for the intended flight plus reserve; verify objects attached or carried are secure and don't affect flight characteristics or controllability.

Waivers & authorizations 107.200, 107.205

Apply through FAA DroneZone (Certificate of Waiver, or airspace authorization). Show how you'll achieve an equivalent level of safety. Apply well in advance — 90 days is the FAA's recommended lead time.

Carriage of property for compensation no single section — 107.25, 107.31 + air carrier limits

  • Allowed under Part 107, with conditions: total weight of aircraft plus payload stays under 55 lb.
  • The flight must be entirely within the boundaries of a single state — no interstate transport, none between Hawaiian islands, none within the District of Columbia.
  • No carriage of property for compensation from a moving vehicle or aircraft.
  • No hazardous materials.

In-flight emergency & inspection authority 107.21, 107.7

  • 107.21: in an in-flight emergency the RPIC may deviate from any rule in Part 107 to the extent necessary to meet that emergency. If asked by the Administrator, you must send a written report of the deviation.
  • 107.7: on request you must present your certificate, registration and any waiver, and make the sUAS available for inspection or testing, to the FAA, NTSB, TSA or any federal, state or local law enforcement officer.
  • The FAA may test or re-examine a certificate holder at any time if there's reason to question qualifications.

Other rules that show up easy points

  • ATC transponders (107.52) and ADS-B Out (107.53) are prohibited on sUAS under Part 107 unless otherwise authorized by the Administrator — they would clutter the system.
  • No sUA may be operated in a manner that interferes with operations and traffic patterns at any airport, heliport or seaplane base.
  • Local and state laws may add restrictions (privacy, trespass, park rules), but only the FAA regulates the airspace itself.
  • Automated or autonomous flight is permitted, but a remote pilot must always be able to take direct control or otherwise terminate flight.

Read the numbers right

Airspace ceilings and floors on sectionals are given in hundreds of feet MSL. So 70 above a line means 7,000 ft MSL, and SFC means surface. Your 400 ft limit is AGL. To compare the two, add the field or terrain elevation to your AGL number. A drone at 400 AGL over ground that sits at 1,200 MSL is at 1,600 MSL.

Getting authorization 107.41 — LAANC & DroneZone

  • Controlled airspace (B, C, D, and E to the surface) requires prior ATC authorization. You cannot call the tower and get verbal permission — the tower isn't the approving authority.
  • LAANC (Low Altitude Authorization and Notification Capability) gives near-real-time approval through an FAA-approved app, up to the altitude ceilings published on the UAS Facility Maps (UASFM).
  • UASFM grids show the maximum altitude the FAA will auto-approve in each square — often 400, 300, 200, 100, or 0 ft. A 0-ft grid means no automatic approval; you must apply through DroneZone for further coordination, which takes ~30–90 days.
  • LAANC also handles further coordination requests and gives you a confirmation you must have available during flight.
  • Recreational flyers also use LAANC, but under a different set of fixed altitudes — don't confuse the two on the test.

Temporary Flight Restrictions 91.137–91.145, 99.7

  • TFRs are published by NOTAM, not printed on sectionals. Check them every flight — tfr.faa.gov, a briefing app, or Flight Service at 1-800-WX-BRIEF.
  • Common triggers: disasters and hazards, aerial demonstrations and airshows, presidential and VIP movement, space operations, firefighting.
  • Stadium TFR: 3 NM radius, up to 3,000 ft AGL, from one hour before to one hour after the event, for MLB, NFL, NCAA Division I football, and major NASCAR races at stadiums seating 30,000 or more.
  • Washington DC has a permanent Special Flight Rules Area (SFRA) with a 15 NM inner Flight Restricted Zone (FRZ) — drones are effectively banned inside the FRZ.

NOTAMs & sources UA.II.B

  • NOTAM = Notice to Air Missions. Categories you may see: NOTAM (D) for distant dissemination, FDC NOTAMs for regulatory items including TFRs and chart changes, and pointer, military and international NOTAMs.
  • Get them from Flight Service (1-800-WX-BRIEF), notams.aim.faa.gov, or an EFB/briefing app.
  • Preflight familiarization also means checking for local flight restrictions, obstacles and the location of people and property — the FAA treats "I didn't know about the TFR" as a preflight failure.

Potential flight hazards near airports UA.II.B

  • Manned aircraft are lowest and slowest near airports — that's where you're most likely to conflict. Stay clear of approach and departure paths and traffic patterns even when you're legally authorized to be there.
  • Wake turbulence: wingtip vortices sink and drift downwind; a light sUAS can be flipped by them. Avoid the area behind and below departing or arriving heavy aircraft.
  • Rotorcraft generate strong downwash and often operate off-pattern at low altitude.
  • Laser illumination of an aircraft is a federal crime; if your aircraft or crew is illuminated, report it to ATC or the FAA.
  • Unlit obstacles: towers, guy wires, cranes, power lines — guy wires are nearly invisible on camera. Survey the site on foot.
Sheet 04 · Area of Operation II

Reading the Sectional

You will be handed chart excerpts in the testing supplement and asked what you're allowed to do at a specific spot. This is the highest-yield hour of study on the whole exam.

Method for every chart question
  • 1. Find the exact point named in the question — usually a town, an airport, or a lat/long.
  • 2. Identify the airspace class at the surface there: solid blue, solid magenta, dashed blue, dashed magenta, magenta vignette, or nothing.
  • 3. Check what's on top of it — a shelf with a floor above 400 AGL doesn't stop you from flying underneath it, but a surface area does mean you need authorization.
  • 4. Sweep for special use airspace, MTRs, obstacles and parachute/glider symbols nearby.
  • 5. Convert: your 400 ft AGL vs. the chart's hundreds-of-feet MSL.

The vignette trap

The two fuzzy bands are the most-missed symbol on the exam. The soft gradient always fades toward the inside of the area it defines, and it marks a Class E floor above the ground — so the surface underneath is Class G and needs no authorization. Only the crisp dashed magenta line brings Class E all the way down, and that one does.

The airport data block read it top to bottom

HOPEDALE MUNI  ← airport name
CT – 118.7  ★  ← control tower freq; star = rotating beacon sunset–sunrise
ATIS 124.6  ← automated terminal information service
241  L  72  122.95  ← field elevation MSL · lighting · longest runway in hundreds of ft · UNICOM

  • Field elevation is always MSL. Runway length is in hundreds of feet, so 72 = 7,200 ft.
  • L = lighting available sunset to sunrise. *L = lighting is part-time or pilot-controlled. No L = no runway lighting.
  • ☉ next to the airport symbol = rotating beacon. NFCT = non-federal control tower.
  • A frequency followed by a small C in a circle is the CTAF — the frequency you monitor at a non-towered field.
  • Blue airport symbol = towered. Magenta airport symbol = non-towered. That one color rule answers a lot of questions.
  • Runways drawn to shape means a hard-surfaced runway 1,500–8,069 ft. A plain circle means services are limited or it's a soft field. Tick marks around the circle = fuel available during normal business hours.
  • R in a box, or "NO SVFR", and other notes appear next to the data block — read them.

Scale, distance and position UA.II.A

  • Sectional charts are drawn at 1:500,000. VFR terminal area charts are 1:250,000 and show more detail around Class B.
  • Latitude runs east–west lines and is measured north/south; longitude runs north–south and is measured east/west. Tick marks along the lines are in minutes; 1 minute of latitude = 1 nautical mile.
  • Use the scale bar in the chart margin or the latitude ticks to estimate distance — a common question format is "how far is X from Y".
  • Chart altitudes and runway headings use magnetic north; the isogonic line (dashed magenta) gives the variation.
Practice this way

Open the FAA-CT-8080-2 testing supplement PDF and work only from the figure numbers, not from an app. Pick a random spot on each sectional excerpt and answer out loud: class at the surface, ceiling and floor, authorization or not, nearest hazard. Twenty repetitions of that drill is worth more than rereading any study guide.

Sheet 05 · Area of Operation III

Weather

Where to get it, how to decode it, and how each phenomenon degrades a small unmanned aircraft specifically.

Sources of weather UA.III.A

  • aviationweather.gov (Aviation Weather Center) — METARs, TAFs, graphical forecasts.
  • Flight Service: 1-800-WX-BRIEF for a standard, abbreviated or outlook briefing. A standard briefing is the full picture; an abbreviated one updates or fills a gap; an outlook is for a flight 6+ hours away.
  • ASOS/AWOS — automated surface/weather observing systems at the field, broadcast on a discrete frequency or by phone. This is your best source for conditions right where you're launching.
  • Only an official source counts for planning. A weather app on your phone is a supplement, not a briefing.
  • Remember that airport observations are taken at the field — microclimates a few miles away can differ, and sUAS fly in the lowest, most turbulent layer no observation describes well.

Ceiling

The ceiling is the lowest BKN or OVC layer, or the vertical visibility into an obscuration. FEW and SCT are not ceilings. In the example above the ceiling is 800 ft — which means you cannot stay 500 ft below the clouds and still fly at any useful altitude.

Decoding a TAF terminal aerodrome forecast

  • Issued four times a day, generally valid for 24 hours (some 30), and applies within about 5 statute miles of the airport.
  • Header format: TAF KBOS 271120Z 2712/2812 — issued the 27th at 1120Z, valid from the 27th at 1200Z to the 28th at 1200Z.
  • Change groups: FM (rapid change at that time, replaces everything after), TEMPO (temporary, less than an hour at a time and less than half the period), BECMG (gradual change), PROB30/40 (probability of the condition occurring).
  • Winds in a TAF, like a METAR, are true north. Winds given by a tower or ATIS over the radio are magnetic.

Atmosphere fundamentals the numbers behind the questions

15°Cstandard sea-level temperature (59°F)
29.92standard sea-level pressure, in. Hg (1013.2 mb)
2°Cstandard temperature lapse per 1,000 ft (3.5°F)
1 in. Hgpressure drop per 1,000 ft of altitude
  • Air moves from high to low pressure. Around a high it flows clockwise and outward (descending, stable, generally good weather); around a low it flows counterclockwise and inward (rising air, clouds, precipitation).
  • The three ingredients for any weather: uneven heating of the Earth's surface, atmospheric pressure differences, and the Coriolis effect from the Earth's rotation.
  • Density altitude = pressure altitude corrected for non-standard temperature. High, hot and humid all raise density altitude, thinning the air. Thin air means less rotor thrust, longer takeoff, weaker climb and shorter endurance — the single most-tested performance concept.

A temperature inversion is warm air sitting above cool air — it caps the atmosphere, trapping haze, smoke and pollutants, and it commonly forms on clear, calm nights. Expect poor visibility at the surface at dawn.

Moisture, fog and clouds UA.III.B

  • Clouds and fog form when the temperature and dew point converge and the air is cooled to saturation with condensation nuclei present. Watch the METAR spread: within 2–3°C is your warning.
  • Radiation fog — clear, calm night, ground cools; classic dawn fog in low areas.
  • Advection fog — moist air moves horizontally over a cooler surface; coastal, and it needs wind so it can persist all day.
  • Upslope fog — moist stable air pushed up rising terrain.
  • Steam fog — cold air over warm water; comes with low-level turbulence.
  • Precipitation-induced fog — warm rain falling into cool air.
  • The base of cumulus clouds can be estimated: divide the temperature/dew point spread in °C by 2.5 and multiply by 1,000 to get feet AGL.

Thunderstorms & wind shear the ones that will destroy the aircraft

  • Three ingredients: sufficient moisture, an unstable lapse rate, and a lifting action.
  • Three stages: cumulus (updrafts building), mature (precipitation at the surface, updrafts and downdrafts — the most hazardous stage), dissipating (downdrafts dominate, anvil top).
  • Never fly within 20 NM of a thunderstorm. Gust fronts run far ahead of the visible storm.
  • Microburst: a violent, concentrated downdraft — downdrafts to 6,000 fpm, horizontal winds up to 45 knots, headwind-to-tailwind shear of up to 90 knots. Typically lasts about 15 minutes. Nothing an sUAS can do about one except not be there.
  • Low-level wind shear can occur with or without a storm: near fronts, near inversions, near terrain. A sudden change in headwind means a sudden change in lift.

The reliable sign a front has passed: the wind shifts. Also expect a pressure change and a temperature/dew point change.

Local winds and how they hit a small aircraft UA.III.B

  • Sea breeze blows from water to land in the afternoon; land breeze reverses at night.
  • Mountain/valley winds: upslope during the day as slopes heat, downslope at night. Expect strong downdrafts on the leeward side of ridges and rotor turbulence downwind of obstacles.
  • Wind flowing over and around buildings creates mechanical turbulence and rotors — the biggest practical hazard for a light drone in an urban site. Expect it up to several times the building's height downwind.
  • Wind reduces endurance sharply: flying downwind out and upwind back can strand you. Plan the return leg into wind and budget reserve.
  • Cold reduces battery capacity; heat degrades battery life and motor performance. Icing on rotors or airframe adds weight and destroys lift.
Sheet 06 · Area of Operation IV

Loading & Performance

The smallest area of the exam and the easiest to bank. It's mostly cause-and-effect: add weight or move the balance, and here is what happens.

Weight UA.IV.A

  • The 55 lb limit is the aircraft plus everything on board or attached at takeoff — battery, payload, camera, gimbal, parachute, mounts.
  • Manufacturer limits are lower and they are the real limit. Never exceed the maximum gross takeoff weight in the operating manual.
  • Effects of an overloaded sUAS: reduced climb rate, reduced maneuverability, higher stall speed for fixed-wing, longer takeoff and landing distance, shorter endurance, greater stress on the airframe, longer stopping distance in a hover-to-stop.
  • An overloaded aircraft is much less able to recover from a gust or an evasive maneuver — the margin you need to yield right of way disappears.

Balance and center of gravity UA.IV.A

  • Center of gravity (CG) is the point where the aircraft would balance. Manufacturers publish CG limits; payloads must be mounted so the CG stays inside them.
  • CG too far forward: nose-heavy; the aircraft pitches down, controls feel heavy, harder to flare or hover level, motors work unevenly.
  • CG too far aft: tail-heavy; unstable and twitchy in pitch, prone to oscillation, hardest to recover.
  • A multirotor compensates for an off-CG load by running some motors harder — that eats battery, generates heat and cuts flight time even when it flies "fine".
  • Adding a payload to one side, or a slung/suspended load, shifts the CG in flight as it swings. Anything carried must be securely attached and must not adversely affect flight characteristics or controllability.
  • If the manufacturer publishes a weight and balance procedure, follow it. If not, load conservatively, test-hover at low altitude, and check the aircraft's behavior before committing to the mission.

Load factor stress in turns

  • Load factor is the ratio of the load supported by the aircraft to its actual weight, expressed in Gs. Straight and level = 1G.
  • Load factor increases with bank angle: 30° ≈ 1.15G, 45° ≈ 1.41G, 60° = 2G. At 60° the aircraft effectively weighs twice as much.
  • Consequences of high load factor: increased stall speed for fixed-wing sUAS, higher power demand, structural stress, and rapid battery drain.
  • Abrupt control inputs and steep turns near obstacles are how small aircraft get away from pilots. Smooth is efficient.

Endurance and reserve plan it, don't guess it

  • Part 107 requires you to verify there is enough power to operate for the intended operational time and to land safely — a reserve is not optional.
  • Published flight times are for a hover, at sea level, in still air, with no payload, on a new battery. Real endurance in wind with a camera is much less.
  • Battery capacity fades with cycle count and age. Track cycles per battery.
  • Plan return-to-home altitude and route before launch, and know the low-battery behavior of your specific aircraft — some auto-land where they are, some climb first.
Sheet 07 · Area of Operation V

Operations — Airport, Crew & Human Factors

The largest area on the exam: radio procedures, airport traffic, decision-making, and what your own body does to your judgment.

Radio communication procedures UA.V.A

  • Part 107 does not require a radio, but you're expected to know how airport communications work so you can monitor and stay out of the way.
  • CTAF (Common Traffic Advisory Frequency) is the frequency pilots self-announce on at a non-towered airport. It may be a UNICOM, MULTICOM or the tower frequency when the tower is closed.
  • UNICOM is a non-government air/ground radio station — usually the FBO — that may provide airport advisories.
  • Self-announce format: who you're calling, who you are, where you are, what you intend to do. Example: "Hopedale traffic, drone operating two miles south of the field, surface to four hundred feet, Hopedale."
  • Manned pilots use the phonetic alphabet (Alfa, Bravo, Charlie, Delta, Echo, Foxtrot, Golf, Hotel, India, Juliett, Kilo, Lima, Mike, November, Oscar, Papa, Quebec, Romeo, Sierra, Tango, Uniform, Victor, Whiskey, X-ray, Yankee, Zulu) and 24-hour Zulu time.
  • At a towered airport, ATC's authorization for your operation comes through LAANC or DroneZone — the tower does not issue it, though you may be asked for a phone number for coordination.

Airport operations UA.V.B — where the manned traffic will be

  • Runway numbers are the magnetic heading rounded to the nearest 10, with the last zero dropped: Runway 27 points about 270°. Opposite ends differ by 18 (e.g. 9 and 27).
  • Aircraft take off and land into the wind. Given a wind of 270° at 15, expect traffic using Runway 27.
  • A segmented circle and a wind sock/tetrahedron near the field show wind and pattern direction. Right-hand traffic is noted with RP in the chart data.
  • Standard pattern altitude for light aircraft is about 1,000 ft AGL, but they are far lower on the approach and departure ends — where you'll be.
  • Never operate in a way that interferes with operations and traffic patterns at any airport, heliport or seaplane base — that's a rule, not advice.
  • Helicopters and medical flights operate off-pattern and often below 500 ft. Hospitals, highways and disaster scenes deserve extra caution.

3P — the loop in flight

  • Perceive the hazard
  • Process the risk
  • Perform the mitigation
  • Risk is assessed on likelihood × severity. A common exam framing: identify the hazard (a condition), then the associated risk (the consequence).
  • Crew resource management (CRM) is the effective use of all available resources — the VO, the client, the manual, ATC, checklists — not just the RPIC's own judgment. Brief every participant before launch: roles, emergency procedures, contingencies, hazards.
  • Situational awareness is an accurate picture of the aircraft, the crew, the environment and the mission. Its enemies are fixation (staring at the camera feed), complacency (the hundredth identical flight), and task saturation.
  • Watch for external pressure: a paying client, a closing weather window, or daylight running out. "Get-there-itis" causes accidents in unmanned aviation exactly as it does in manned.

Physiology UA.V.E

  • Vision: the eye's central cone vision is best in daylight; the peripheral rods do the work at night. At night, use off-center viewing — scan 10–15° off to the side of the object, because the center of your vision has a night blind spot. Full dark adaptation takes about 30 minutes, and bright white light destroys it in seconds.
  • Scanning for traffic: use a series of short, regularly spaced eye movements in roughly 10° sectors, pausing at each to focus. An aircraft on a collision course shows no apparent relative motion — it just grows larger in one spot on your windscreen.
  • Dehydration causes headache, drowsiness, dizziness and impaired judgment. Heat, wind and sun on a field site produce it fast. Heatstroke follows if untreated.
  • Hyperventilation from stress produces dizziness, tingling, and a sense of suffocation. Fix it by slowing the breathing rate and talking aloud.
  • Stress and fatigue narrow attention and degrade decision quality. Acute fatigue comes from a hard day; chronic fatigue from sustained sleep debt and does not go away with one night's rest.
  • Drugs and alcohol: 8 hours minimum after drinking, BAC under 0.04, and no medication that affects your faculties. Antihistamines, sleep aids and cold remedies are common offenders. When in doubt, don't fly.
  • Hypoxia and spatial disorientation matter less to a pilot standing on the ground — but questions on the general effects of altitude and illness still appear.

Crew, site and security UA.V.C

  • The RPIC is the final authority and must be designated before every flight. A person may manipulate the controls under the RPIC's direct supervision if the RPIC can immediately take direct control.
  • A visual observer must be able to communicate with the RPIC at all times by an effective means — voice, radio, or hand signals arranged in advance.
  • Run a site survey: launch and recovery zones, obstacles, overhead wires, terrain, people, vehicle traffic, RF interference, emergency landing areas, and the nearest way out.
  • Brief everyone: mission, roles, hazards, lost-link plan, abort criteria, who calls the abort (anyone should be able to).
  • Be alert to GPS interference near tall structures, canyons and metallic structures, and to intentional jamming or spoofing. Know how your aircraft behaves in ATTI mode with no GPS.
  • Cyber and data security: keep firmware current, control who has access to the control station, and treat imagery of people and property with care — privacy is regulated by state law, not the FAA.
Sheet 08 · Area of Operation V

Maintenance, Emergencies & Night

The rest of Area V: keeping the aircraft in a condition for safe operation, and what to do when it stops being in one.

Maintenance & inspection UA.V.F

  • There is no FAA-mandated maintenance schedule for Part 107 aircraft and no airworthiness certificate. The manufacturer's recommended schedule is the standard, and if there isn't one, you establish your own based on how the aircraft is used.
  • Scheduled maintenance is done on a calendar or cycle interval. Unscheduled maintenance is triggered by a discrepancy — a hard landing, a warning, unusual vibration.
  • Who may perform it: the remote pilot or owner may do the maintenance themselves if qualified. If the work is beyond your ability or the manufacturer requires it, use the manufacturer or a qualified technician. There is no A&P requirement for sUAS — but the RPIC still owns the decision that the aircraft is safe.
  • Record keeping is not required by Part 107 but is strongly recommended: dates, flight hours or cycles, discrepancies found, corrective action, who did it, and the aircraft it applies to. Records matter for insurance, for clients, and if the FAA ever investigates.
  • If the aircraft cannot be returned to a condition for safe operation, ground it. Never fly a known discrepancy because the mission is scheduled.

Preflight inspection every flight, every time

Aircraft

  • Airframe for cracks, loose arms and fasteners
  • Propellers for nicks, chips, warping; correct rotation direction and matched set
  • Motors spin freely, no grinding, no debris
  • Battery: charge level, no swelling or puffing, no damage, secure latch and connectors
  • Payload and gimbal securely attached, lens clear, CG within limits
  • Firmware current, storage card seated and with capacity
  • Anti-collision lighting for night ops, visible 3 SM

Control station & environment

  • Controller charged, sticks and switches free, antennas positioned
  • Control link and video link verified before launch
  • GPS lock, home point set, RTH altitude above the tallest obstacle
  • Compass and IMU calibration if the site or aircraft calls for it
  • Airspace and authorization confirmed; NOTAMs and TFRs checked
  • Weather at the site; wind at altitude, not just at the ground
  • Crew briefed; bystanders and hazards identified; landing zone clear

Batteries the most common source of trouble

  • Lithium polymer cells are damaged by over-discharge, over-charge, physical impact and heat. A puffed or swollen pack is retired, not flown.
  • Charge and store at room temperature, on a non-flammable surface, unattended charging avoided. Store at partial (storage) charge, not full, for long periods.
  • Cold weather cuts usable capacity significantly — pre-warm packs and expect earlier low-voltage warnings.
  • A LiPo fire is a chemical fire that self-sustains. Carry a suitable extinguisher and a fire-resistant bag; plan to smother and isolate rather than to save the pack.
  • Track cycles and retire packs on the manufacturer's schedule. Battery failure is the leading cause of unplanned landings and flyaways.

Emergency procedures UA.V.C, 107.21

SituationResponse
Lost link / loss of controlKnow your aircraft's programmed behavior in advance — hover, return to home, or land. Move to regain the link, keep visual contact, warn people beneath the aircraft, and be ready to terminate the flight.
FlyawayAttempt to regain control and to terminate flight; alert bystanders; note the direction and last known position. If it enters controlled airspace or causes injury or damage, report it.
Low batteryLand immediately at the nearest safe spot. Do not try to make it back across a hazard on a warning.
Loss of GPSExpect drift. Fly manually (ATTI), reduce altitude, and land if you cannot hold position.
Manned aircraft approachingYou always yield. Descend and land, or move well clear. A low helicopter gives you almost no warning.
In-flight emergency107.21 lets you deviate from any Part 107 rule to the extent necessary to meet the emergency. If the Administrator requests it, submit a written report of the deviation.
After an accident

Report to the FAA within 10 calendar days if there was serious injury, loss of consciousness, or at least $500 in damage to property other than the aircraft. Preserve the aircraft, the logs and the flight data — and don't post about it before you understand what happened.

Night operations checklist 107.29

  • Anti-collision lighting visible for 3 statute miles, flash rate sufficient to avoid collision, on for the whole flight in darkness or civil twilight.
  • Preflight in daylight if possible, or with adequate lighting. Walk the site before dark to find wires, poles and drop-offs.
  • Give yourself 30 minutes to dark-adapt; use red or low-intensity white light at the control station.
  • Use off-center viewing to spot the aircraft and other traffic; a light directly ahead may be invisible in your night blind spot.
  • Learn manned aircraft lighting so you can tell where the traffic is going: red on the left wingtip, green on the right, white at the tail. Red and green together with white below means it's coming toward you.
  • Depth perception is poor at night — rely on the aircraft's telemetry for altitude and distance, and keep the mission simple.

Traps that catch people read the question twice

  • AGL vs MSL. Your limit is AGL; the chart is MSL. Add the field elevation before you compare.
  • Hundreds of feet. A 25 in a dashed box is 2,500 ft, not 25 ft. A runway 72 is 7,200 ft.
  • Zulu time. Convert before you answer a "what time locally" question, and mind daylight saving.
  • True vs magnetic wind. METAR and TAF winds are true; tower and ATIS winds spoken over the radio are magnetic.
  • Night is legal now. Any answer that says you need a waiver for night flight is a stale answer — you need anti-collision lighting instead.
  • The tower can't authorize you. Authorization comes through LAANC or DroneZone, even if you can see the controller from where you're standing.
  • A shelf overhead isn't a wall. Under a Class B or C shelf whose floor is above you, the surface airspace may well be Class G — no authorization needed. Check what's at the surface.
  • "Sustained flight" vs "transiting" decides the ops-over-people answers. So does whether it's an open-air assembly.
  • Serious injury and $500 are two separate triggers for the same 10-day report. Damage to your own drone doesn't count.
  • Hazmat and the 55 lb limit cannot be waived. Most of the operating limitations can.
  • When two answers look right, pick the one that is more conservative and more specific to the regulation. The FAA's answer is almost never the aggressive one.
